# B2B Payments Platform

Companies pay their suppliers by card: the platform issues a single-use virtual card for each approved invoice, approves the supplier's charge in real time, and reconciles every cent the next day. This repo builds the card issuer's side of that system.

It's a learning project modelled on a senior payments engineering role (virtual cards, payables, event-driven services). Every significant decision is recorded with its reasoning, so the docs matter as much as the code.

> **Status:** M6 (everything runs on Kubernetes) is done: steps 1 to 6 below work end to end, reconciliation included. An ERP's invoice is approved by one or two approvers and paid with a single-use card whose full number lives only in the encrypted vault; the supplier opens the card's details once through its remittance advice link and charges the card. The simulated card network asks `authorization-service` over gRPC, a separate Spring Boot app with its own database that approves or declines the charge from its own copy of the cards, so charges are still approved while the platform is stopped. The platform and the service keep each other informed over Kafka, each through an outbox so no event is lost: the platform sends every card it issues, cancels, expires or clears, and a payment waits until the service has its card before the supplier gets it; the service sends every approved and reversed authorization, from which the platform keeps its own copy. When the service's database is slow or down, the service answers `91` (issuer unavailable) within milliseconds instead of letting the network time out, and reverses anything that committed anyway. If no answer comes within a second, the network declines the charge and sends a reversal. The network's daily clearing file is imported by a Spring Batch job, once the platform's copy of the authorizations has caught up, that posts every charge to a double-entry ledger, records how each matched its authorization, and pays the network the file's total; a broken or repeated file is refused whole, a repeated record is skipped, and an import that stops restarts where it stopped. What the buyer owes counts against its credit until the issuer records its repayment. A card nobody charges within 30 days, or whose charge doesn't clear within 7 days, expires and frees its credit. A daily reconciliation turns every mismatch (an undercharge, a clearing for a card nobody issued, an authorization that never cleared, an unpaid invoice, a settlement that disagrees with the network, books out of balance) into a Break, which a Reconciler resolves by accepting it or by posting a correcting entry that moves the money where it belongs. Pact contract tests check every event both ways, and one trace runs from the network's call through the service and Kafka to the platform. Grafana shows four dashboards and alerts when the authorization p99 stays above 100 ms, when a payment waits more than 5 minutes for its card to be on file, and when the platform or the service stops sending metrics. M4's Gatling load test at 200 authorizations a second, run unchanged against the service, measured a server-side p99 of 16.2 ms, and 8.2 ms with the databases on storage with btrfs copy-on-write off; one run each, consistent with the disk setting the tail as in M4 (35.6 and 104.2 ms against the platform) ([measured](docs/design/m5-authorization-service.md#measured-97)). Once the apps are running, three commands run it, switches on the second show every clearing edge case, and one more resolves a Break (see [Run it locally](#run-it-locally)). The same stack also runs as a platform: one Helm chart installs everything on a single-node k3d cluster, with two pods each of the platform, `authorization-service` and a Spring Cloud Gateway that now stands in front of the platform's API, checking tokens and limiting each client's rate through Redis (a client over its limit gets `429`; with Redis down, requests still pass and Grafana alerts). Any of the three apps can be redeployed without losing a request: CI's load test rolls all three, one pod at a time, while the network charges cards and an ERP submits invoices, and fails on a single failed call. At 200 authorizations a second through those three rollouts, the cluster measured a server-side p99 of 13.4 ms with no failed call ([measured](docs/design/m6-run-as-a-platform.md#measured-119); see [Run it on Kubernetes](#run-it-on-kubernetes)). Next is M7: the card vault becomes its own service. See the [roadmap](#roadmap).

## How a payment works

Acme (a Buyer) owes Globex (a Supplier) $12,400.

1. Acme's ERP sends Globex's invoice to the platform.
2. An Acme approver approves it. Invoices above Acme's approval threshold need two different approvers.
3. The platform reserves $12,400 of Acme's credit and issues a single-use virtual card locked to Globex and that amount.
4. Globex receives a remittance advice with a one-time link to the card details.
5. Globex charges the card. The card network asks `authorization-service`, the Issuer's own authorization service, to approve the charge, and it answers within 100 ms.
6. The next day, the network's clearing file confirms the final amount, the platform settles with the network, and reconciliation flags anything that doesn't match for a person to resolve.

The card network, Globex's bank and Acme's ERP are simulators. The platform is the real system.

## What makes it hard

- **Money must add up.** A double-entry ledger records every movement, and a retried request never charges twice.
- **Real-time decisions.** 99% of card approvals are answered within 100 ms.
- **Payments that fail halfway.** Paying an invoice spans several steps; a saga undoes the earlier ones when a later one fails.
- **Card data handled as if PCI DSS applied.** Full card numbers live in one encrypted vault; everything else handles tokens.
- **Systems that disagree.** The network's records and ours are reconciled daily, and every mismatch becomes a Break for a person to resolve.

## Tech

Java 25, Spring Boot 4, Maven, PostgreSQL, Spring Modulith, gRPC, Spring Batch, Keycloak, OpenTelemetry with Grafana, Kafka with Pact contract tests between the platform and `authorization-service`, and, in the service, WebFlux with R2DBC and Resilience4j. M6 runs it all on Kubernetes (k3d and Helm) behind Spring Cloud Gateway, which limits each client's rate in Redis. A later milestone adds Spring AI. Each technology arrives in the milestone where the system first needs it, and the [decision records](docs/adr/) say why.

## Run it locally

You need Java 25 (Corretto), Docker running, OpenSSL, and ports 5432, 5433, 3000, 4317, 4318, 8080, 8081, 8082, 8083, 8180, 6379 and 9090 free. Docker Compose starts the database, the telemetry stack, Keycloak and the Gateway's Redis for you.

1. Once, generate local secrets: `./scripts/init-env.sh`. It writes random development secrets into `.env`, which git ignores; running it again keeps existing values and adds any new ones. After pulling a change to `keycloak/payments-realm.json` (such as a new client), run it again, then `docker compose up -d --force-recreate keycloak`: Keycloak imports the realm only into a fresh container. If your `.env` predates the card vault or the Remittance Advice, run it again too: it adds the vault's two keys (`VAULT_ENCRYPTION_KEY`, `VAULT_HASH_KEY`) and the Remittance Advice link key (`REMITTANCE_LINK_KEY`), without which the platform refuses to start. M2 adds the Network's Keycloak client: run it again for `NETWORK_CLIENT_SECRET`, then recreate Keycloak as above. M4 adds the `simulator-reconciler` client, which resolves Breaks: run it again for `SIMULATOR_RECONCILER_SECRET`, then recreate Keycloak.
2. Start the platform: `./mvnw -pl platform -am spring-boot:run`. The first start pulls the PostgreSQL, Grafana LGTM and Keycloak images, then the platform listens on <http://localhost:8080> for REST. Then, in another terminal, start M5's `authorization-service` beside it: `./mvnw -pl authorization-service -am spring-boot:run`. Either may start first. The service answers the Network's gRPC calls on `127.0.0.1:9090`, which need the `network` client's token, from its own copy of the cards, kept from the platform's `cards` topic in its own PostgreSQL; it reports its health on <http://localhost:8082/actuator/health>. The platform keeps a copy of the service's approved and reversed Authorizations, from the `authorizations` topic, for Clearing and Reconciliation. Payments need the service: each one waits, its card issued, until the service says it has the card on file, and only then sends the Supplier its Remittance Advice; without the service, a Payment stays `CARD_ISSUED` ([`docs/runbooks/payment-stuck.md`](docs/runbooks/payment-stuck.md)), and the Network declines every Charge itself with `91`.

   Then, in a third terminal, start M6's Gateway in front of the platform: `./mvnw -pl gateway spring-boot:run`. It listens on <http://localhost:8083> and sends every `/v1` request on to the platform at `8080`, `Authorization` header and all, once it has checked the Keycloak token: without a valid one it answers `401` itself, except for the remittance link, which needs none. With a valid token, calls through `8083` get the platform's own answers, so the steps below call the platform directly; the Gateway answers some errors itself, as Problem Details: `404` for a path outside `/v1` and `502` while the platform is down; try `curl -s -i http://localhost:8083/v1/invoices` for the Gateway's `401`. It also limits each client's rate through Compose's Redis: past a burst of 40, a client gets `429` with `Retry-After` until its bucket refills at 20 a second, and the remittance link allows each address a burst of 10, then one every 6 seconds (10 a minute). Its health is on <http://localhost:8083/actuator/health>.

   Coming from an earlier milestone? Reset local data first with `docker compose down -v`: the platform's Authorizations from before the cutover and the service's copy of the cards would disagree.
3. In another terminal, start the simulator: `./mvnw -pl simulator -am spring-boot:run`. It listens on <http://localhost:8081>, reuses the running stack, and creates the Buyers Acme (the demo's) and Initech (the load test's) through the platform, signed in through Keycloak. Its card network reaches the service's gRPC server on `localhost:9090`, signed in as the `network` client with `NETWORK_CLIENT_SECRET` from `.env` (step 1).
4. Run the whole payment in one call: `curl -s -X POST http://localhost:8081/demo/invoices`. The simulator's ERP submits a $12,400 invoice from Globex, two of Acme's approvers approve it (it is above Acme's $10,000 threshold), and the simulator waits for the platform to pay it. Globex then charges the card: its acquirer sends the charge through the simulator's card network, which asks `authorization-service` over gRPC. Within a second or two the demo answers with the invoice, who approved it, `"paymentState": "COMPLETED"`, what Globex received through its one-time link (the card's last four digits, expiry and amount, never the full number) and the service's answer to the charge: `"authorization": {"approved": true, "responseCode": "00", "authorizationCode": "…"}`. If the service doesn't answer within a second, or can't be reached, the network declines the charge itself with `"responseCode": "91"` and reverses it; see [`docs/runbooks/network-cant-reach-platform.md`](docs/runbooks/network-cant-reach-platform.md). Add `?minorUnits=500000` for a $5,000 invoice, which takes one approval. Each paid invoice takes its amount of Acme's $100,000 credit line, reserved until its charge clears and then owed until Acme repays it (Issuer staff record that with `POST /v1/buyers/{buyerId}/repayments`), so the ninth $12,400 demo answers `"paymentState": "FAILED"` with `"Not enough Available Credit to place the Hold."`. `docker compose down` followed by restarting the apps starts again with an empty database. For any other payment that isn't `COMPLETED`, see [`docs/runbooks/payment-stuck.md`](docs/runbooks/payment-stuck.md).
5. Clear the charges: `curl -s -X POST localhost:8081/demo/clearing-file`. Globex's acquirer submitted each approved charge for clearing; now the simulator's card network writes today's clearing file with every one it hasn't sent yet into `var/clearing`, the platform's drop folder, and the simulator, signed in as Issuer staff, asks the platform to import it at once. The answer is the file's name and the platform's answer to the import, its summary, such as `{"fileName": "NET-20261016-7K2QX9A.txt", "imports": [{"status": 201, "summary": {"id": "NET-20261016-7K2QX9A", "sequenceNumber": 1, "status": "IMPORTED", "recordCount": 1, "outcomes": {"matched": 1, …}, "settlement": {…, "matched": true}}}]}`; with nothing to clear it writes no file and answers `204` with no body. Without the call, the network writes the file for each day at 00:05 UTC and the platform's 30-second check of the folder imports it. The network keeps the charges it hasn't sent, and its count of files sent, in memory only: restarting the simulator loses them, and its next file is number 1 again.

   To watch each of the platform's clearing rules at work, charge a card again (step 4), then add switches, which change only that call's file: `duplicateRecord` sends a record twice (the platform skips the repeat, `"skippedCount": 1`, and records the settlement as mismatched, as the network settles the record twice); `aboveAuthorizedAmount` clears the charge $100 above what the platform authorized; `noAuthorization` adds a clearing on the card that no authorization matches; `unknownPan` adds one for a card the platform never issued, which it posts to Clearing Suspense; `wrongTrailerTotal` puts the file's control total a cent out, so the platform refuses the whole file (`"status": 422`); and `sameFileTwice` sends the file twice, so the second import answers `409` (or `422` again beside `wrongTrailerTotal`: a refused file doesn't count as imported). Each import's outcome counts or problem show in `imports`. Combine switches with `&`: `curl -s -X POST 'localhost:8081/demo/clearing-file?duplicateRecord=true&noAuthorization=true&unknownPan=true'`. A refused or repeated file waits in `var/clearing/failed/` and holds the cards' full numbers: delete it once you've seen it ([`docs/runbooks/clearing-file-refused.md`](docs/runbooks/clearing-file-refused.md)). The network doesn't send a refused file's charges again, so their credit stays reserved until the 7-day rule frees it.
6. Reconcile today: `curl -s -X POST localhost:8081/demo/reconciliation`. The simulator, signed in as Issuer staff, asks the platform to run today's Reconciliation at once and lists today's open Breaks. The answer is the run's summary and each Break's ID, kind, gap and Buyer (`null` when none is known), such as `{"run": {"runId": "…", "businessDate": "2026-10-16", "breaksFound": 1, "newBreaks": 1, "knownBreaks": 0}, "openBreaks": [{"id": "…", "kind": "UNKNOWN_CARD", "gap": {"minorUnits": 1240000, "currency": "USD"}, "buyerId": null}]}`. After a plain step 5 it finds nothing to raise: `"breaksFound": 0` and no open Breaks. Running it again raises nothing twice: a Break it found before counts in `knownBreaks`. Without the call, the platform reconciles each day at 00:05 UTC the next day; if a run fails, see [`docs/runbooks/reconciliation-run-failed.md`](docs/runbooks/reconciliation-run-failed.md).

   To resolve a Break, charge a card (step 4), clear it with `curl -s -X POST 'localhost:8081/demo/clearing-file?unknownPan=true'`, and reconcile (above): the platform posted the unknown card's clearing to Clearing Suspense, and the answer shows its `UNKNOWN_CARD` Break with no Buyer. Say Acme turns out to owe it. As a Reconciler, the `simulator-reconciler` client signed in with `SIMULATOR_RECONCILER_SECRET` from `.env`, reassign it to Acme (Buyer `dc9a9857-e40f-4d50-9ee3-879f0c7472c5`) for the Break's gap. From the repository root, with `jq` installed, put the Break's `id` from the answer in `break_id`, and if its `gap` isn't the default charge's 1240000, put its `minorUnits` in `amount` instead:

   ```sh
   break_id=<the UNKNOWN_CARD Break's id>
   secret=$(grep '^SIMULATOR_RECONCILER_SECRET=' .env | cut -d= -f2-)
   token=$(curl -s -d grant_type=client_credentials -d client_id=simulator-reconciler \
     --data-urlencode "client_secret=$secret" \
     http://localhost:8180/realms/payments/protocol/openid-connect/token | jq -r .access_token)
   curl -s -X POST http://localhost:8080/v1/breaks/$break_id/resolution \
     -H "Authorization: Bearer $token" -H 'Content-Type: application/json' \
     -d '{"kind": "REASSIGNED", "buyerId": "dc9a9857-e40f-4d50-9ee3-879f0c7472c5",
          "amount": {"minorUnits": 1240000, "currency": "USD"},
          "note": "Acme confirmed the charge on the unknown card"}'
   ```

   The answer is the Break, `"status": "RESOLVED"`, with a `resolution` naming the Reconciler, the note and the `correctingEntryId`: a Correcting Entry debited Acme's Buyer Receivable and credited Clearing Suspense, so Acme now owes the amount. `curl -s http://localhost:8080/v1/breaks/$break_id -H "Authorization: Bearer $token"` shows it `RESOLVED` too, with the facts of the clearing it names; `GET /v1/breaks?status=OPEN` lists the rest. Resolving it again answers `409`, so money moves once. A Break is also resolved as `ACCEPTED` (a note, no money moves) or `CORRECTED` (a balanced Correcting Entry between any two Accounts); see `POST /v1/breaks/{breakId}/resolution` in [the API](contracts/src/main/resources/openapi/platform-api.yaml) and [how Reconciliation works](docs/learning/reconciliation.md). Issuer staff can read Breaks but not resolve them.
7. Open Grafana at <http://localhost:3000> (no login). Under **Dashboards > B2B Payments**, **Authorization** shows the Network's calls to `authorization-service`, their p50/p95/p99 latency against the 100 ms target, the answers by response code and the Kafka consumer lag on both topics; **Payments** the saga states Payments reach, how long each step runs and the Payments waiting for their card to be on file; **Clearing** the files imported or refused, Clearings by outcome and Settlements matched or mismatched; **Breaks** the open Breaks by kind and by age. **Alerting > Alert rules** holds four rules, which notify no one: the Authorization p99 above 100 ms for 5 minutes, a Payment waiting more than 5 minutes for its card to be on file, and the platform or `authorization-service` sending no metrics for 5 minutes; [the load test](#the-load-test) is how to put that p99 under load. They come from `grafana/` in this repository ([how to edit them](docs/learning/grafana-provisioning.md)). Under **Explore**, pick **Tempo** for traces, **Loki** for logs or **Prometheus** for metrics. Metrics arrive once a minute. In Tempo, the TraceQL query `{name="http post /demo/invoices"}` finds each demo call as one trace across the simulator and the platform, and `{ name = "payments.network.v1.IssuerAuthorization/Authorize" && kind = server }` finds each Charge's trace, from the Network's call through `authorization-service` and Kafka to the platform's copy.
8. Keycloak's admin console is at <http://localhost:8180>: user `admin`, password `KEYCLOAK_ADMIN_PASSWORD` from `.env`.
9. Stopping an app leaves the stack running for the other. Stop the stack with `docker compose down`. If only part of it is running, run `docker compose up -d` before starting an app: Spring Boot starts the stack only when none of it is running.

### Cards for the load test

The load test charges fresh cards that the platform issued through its real path. With the platform, `authorization-service` and the simulator running (steps 2 and 3), seed them with `curl -s -X POST 'localhost:8081/demo/load-test-cards?count=1000'` (`count` from 1 to 25,000). Initech's ERP sends that many $10.00 invoices to Globex, Initech's approver approves each (Initech's threshold is $100, so one approval is enough), the simulator waits for each payment, and Globex opens each card's one-time link. The answer is `{"requested": 1000, "written": 1000, "file": "…/var/load-test/cards.csv"}`; `written` is lower if some payments failed or took over two minutes, and the simulator's log says why. Add `&append=true` to add the cards to the file instead of overwriting it. The simulator keeps 64 invoices in flight, and the platform's payment worker runs every due payment's steps each second, on `payables.payment-worker.threads` workers (4), each payment pausing until `authorization-service` has its card on file. The seeding rate hasn't been measured since M5 added that wait. Initech's $1,000,000 credit line covers 100,000 such cards before Issuer staff need to record a repayment.

`var/load-test/cards.csv` holds one card per line under the header `pan,securityCode,expiry,amountMinorUnits,currency,acceptorId`, the expiry as `YYMM`. Git ignores it, but it holds the cards' full numbers and security codes: delete it after the load test.

### The load test

The `load-test` module's Gatling simulation measures `authorization-service`'s answer to the Network's `Authorize` against the 100 ms target (ADR-0010). CI never runs it: it compiles there, but a p99 on a shared runner means nothing.

1. Start the stack, the platform, `authorization-service` and the simulator (steps 1 to 3), with nothing else busy on the machine: a build or test run beside it moves the p99.
2. Seed 28,000 fresh cards, the most one call takes being 25,000: `curl -s -X POST 'localhost:8081/demo/load-test-cards?count=25000'`, then `curl -s -X POST 'localhost:8081/demo/load-test-cards?count=3000&append=true'`. The run charges about 27,300 of them, each fresh card once, so seed again before every run.
3. Run it, signed in as the Network with the secret from `.env`: `NETWORK_CLIENT_SECRET=$(grep '^NETWORK_CLIENT_SECRET=' .env | cut -d= -f2-) ./mvnw -B -pl load-test -am -Pload-test verify`. It sends 200 Authorizations a second for a 30-second warm-up, then for 2 measured minutes, from 5 users that each start a Charge every 25 ms: Gatling's open-source gRPC support stops a run past 5 users ([why](docs/learning/gatling.md)), so a slow answer lowers the achieved rate as well as showing as latency. About 90% charge a fresh card and expect `00`; 5% charge a card already charged (`57`) and 5% send a wrong security code (`N7`). Any other answer or a gRPC error fails the call, and one failed call fails the build. `-Drate=20 -DwarmUpSeconds=5 -DmeasuredSeconds=10` makes a short trial run; `-DcardsFile=…` charges another file.
4. Read the results. Gatling's client-side numbers are in the console and in the HTML report under `load-test/target/gatling/`: the three **Measured: …** rows are the 2-minute window, apart from the **Warm-up: …** rows; check their requests per second add up to about 200. The service's own server-side p50/p95/p99 are on Grafana's **Authorization** dashboard; set the time range to the measured window. The 100 ms target is the server-side p99. The [M4 design](docs/design/m4-reconciliation-and-measurement.md#measured-75) records the platform's measured runs, the [M5 design](docs/design/m5-authorization-service.md#measured-97) the service's, and the [M6 design](docs/design/m6-run-as-a-platform.md#measured-119) the cluster's.
5. Delete the cards file: `rm var/load-test/cards.csv`.

The module's second simulation, `InvoiceSimulation`, has Acme's ERP submit $10.00 Invoices, each under its own `Idempotency-Key`, to a Supplier only it uses; they stay `RECEIVED`. The cluster runs it through redeploys ("Run it on Kubernetes", step 8), and it runs from the host too, against the Gateway on the cluster's `localhost:8080` or the platform on Compose's: `ACME_ERP_SECRET=$(grep '^ACME_ERP_SECRET=' .env | cut -d= -f2-) ./mvnw -B -pl load-test -am -Pload-test verify -Dgatling.simulationClass=dev.rgonz.payments.loadtest.InvoiceSimulation`. It sends 5 a second for 60 seconds (`-Drate`, `-DdurationSeconds`, at most 240; `-DbaseUrl` for another address), and any answer but `201` fails the build. Through the Gateway, Acme's ERP gets 20 requests a second.

How to read the report and what tripped us up: [`docs/learning/gatling.md`](docs/learning/gatling.md).

Tests need Docker but not the stack or `.env`: `./mvnw verify` starts its own throwaway PostgreSQL, Kafka and Keycloak. Among them, the end-to-end test starts the platform and the simulator in one JVM and `authorization-service` beside them from its executable jar, then runs the demo and a small load-test seeding through them; a second test stops the platform, charges cards through the service alone and checks the platform catches up once restarted; `./mvnw -B -pl e2e -am verify` builds and tests the three apps, then runs it.

## Run it on Kubernetes

The same stack, run as a platform (M6): everything on a single-node [k3d](https://k3d.io) cluster from one Helm chart, with the Gateway in front of the platform ([Deployment](docs/architecture.md#deployment)). Compose and `spring-boot:run` above stay the development loop. You need Java 25, Docker, OpenSSL, k3d 5.8, Helm and kubectl, and ports 8080, 8081 and 8180 free: stop the apps and `docker compose down` first. From the repository root:

1. Generate local secrets, if you haven't: `./scripts/init-env.sh` (step 1 above).
2. Build the apps' images with buildpacks (the first build pulls the builder, a few minutes): `./mvnw -B -pl platform,authorization-service,simulator,gateway -am -DskipTests package spring-boot:build-image`, the command CI's `k3d` job runs. `package` comes first because `build-image` alone doesn't package `contracts`, which the apps need, on a clean checkout. It makes `b2b-payments/platform:local` and the like in your Docker.
3. Create the cluster and import the images: `./scripts/k3d-cluster.sh`. It creates the k3d cluster `b2b-payments` (k3s's Traefik off, this repository mounted into its node, the host's `127.0.0.1:8080`, `8081` and `8180` mapped to it), switches `kubectl` to it and imports the four images. After rebuilding an image, run it again and `kubectl rollout restart deployment/<app>`.
4. Turn `.env` into Kubernetes Secrets: `./scripts/k8s-secrets.sh`. Each app gets a Secret with only the variables it reads, and Keycloak one with the admin password and every client secret, for its realm; nothing secret is in the chart.
5. Install the chart, running the apps that write under `var/` as you: `helm upgrade --install b2b-payments deploy/helm/b2b-payments --set global.hostUser.uid=$(id -u) --set global.hostUser.gid=$(id -g)`. `kubectl get pods -w` shows them start; wait until all thirteen are `1/1 Running` (`authorization-service`, the platform and the Gateway run two each); an app that starts before its database restarts once or twice (if one isn't: [`pod-not-ready.md`](docs/runbooks/pod-not-ready.md)).
6. Run the demo as in steps 4 to 6 above, on the same addresses: `curl -s -X POST localhost:8081/demo/invoices`, then `curl -s -X POST localhost:8081/demo/clearing-file` and `curl -s -X POST localhost:8081/demo/reconciliation`. The simulator now reaches the platform through the Gateway, its Network reaches `authorization-service` over gRPC inside the cluster, and the Clearing File goes through `var/clearing` in this repository, which both pods mount.
7. Call the platform through the Gateway on <http://localhost:8080> with a token from Keycloak on <http://localhost:8180>, as in step 6's Break resolution above: tokens name `http://localhost:8180` as their issuer, and the apps fetch Keycloak's keys inside the cluster. Grafana: `kubectl port-forward service/lgtm 3000`, then <http://localhost:3000>.
8. Run the load test through a redeploy of `authorization-service`, the platform and the Gateway: `./scripts/k8s-load-test.sh`. It needs the chart installed with `-f deploy/helm/b2b-payments/load-test-values.yaml` added to step 5, which points the simulator past the Gateway, whose rate limits a seeding of thousands of cards would hit. It seeds the cards, then runs both Gatling simulations inside the cluster as a Job from this repository: 50 Authorizations a second straight to the service, and Acme's ERP submitting 5 Invoices a second through the Gateway, each under its own `Idempotency-Key`, both for 180 seconds (`RATE`, `INVOICE_RATE` and `DURATION` change them; `WARM_UP` adds a warm-up before them, which the rollouts wait out; the two together at most 240). While they run it restarts the service's, then the platform's, then the Gateway's two pods, one pod at a time, each app once the last one's old pods are gone. It fails on any failed call in either simulation; then it prints how many Charges each service pod answered. `--no-restart` runs them without the redeploys. The first run downloads Maven's dependencies into `var/maven`; CI's `k3d` job runs the same. The measured run, at M4's profile, is `RATE=200 WARM_UP=30 DURATION=120 ./scripts/k8s-load-test.sh` on a fresh cluster whose step 5 also has `--set authorization-service.env.MANAGEMENT_OTLP_METRICS_EXPORT_STEP=10s --set platform.env.MANAGEMENT_OTLP_METRICS_EXPORT_STEP=10s`, so Prometheus gets the service's histogram every 10 seconds; seeding its 30,000 cards takes about 18 minutes, and the M6 design's [Measured (#119)](docs/design/m6-run-as-a-platform.md#measured-119) says how its figures were read. Delete `var/load-test/cards.csv` afterwards, as above. From a cluster installed before #116, `kubectl delete service authorization-service` before upgrading: it became headless, which Kubernetes can't change in place.
9. `helm uninstall b2b-payments` removes the stack but keeps the databases' and Kafka's volumes; `k3d cluster delete b2b-payments` removes everything.
